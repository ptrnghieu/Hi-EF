\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{abstract}
Emotion forecasting asks how a person will feel in the next conversational turn, before that turn is observed.
In the Hi-EF benchmark the next speaker is not identified, and the published model fuses each preceding clip as a
whole, so the evidence is dominated by whoever is speaking. We observe that Hi-EF is multi-party video edited in shot
and reverse shot: the person who answers is usually already on screen, listening. We therefore make the
\emph{participant}, rather than the clip, the unit of evidence. \model{} groups the faces of a sample into people
without any identity information, summarises each person in each turn by one token, and reads these tokens together
with speech and scene tokens in a small set encoder (0.71M parameters). On the locked Hi-EF test split, evaluated once
under a preregistered plan, \model{} improves unweighted average recall over a retrained version of the strongest
published model by 3.84 points and wins seven of eight test episodes; the preregistered primary contrast, under logit
adjustment, was not significant, mainly because the test set holds six examples of one class. Controlled analyses
show what matters and what does not. The faces of co-present participants and the earlier turns are worth about two
points each, whereas which participant is taken as the likely responder, the true responder's identity and the
role labels of the slots make no measurable difference. The remaining errors lie in recognising participants'
emotional states, not in how their evidence is combined.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recognising an emotion that is displayed is a perception problem; forecasting one that has not yet been displayed is
a problem about people. Affective forecasting has a long history in psychology \citep{wilson2003affective}, and
\citet{wang2026hief} turned a version of it into a benchmark: given three consecutive clips of a TV conversation
(I--III), predict the emotion of the person who speaks in clip IV. The task has two unusual properties. The target
clip is never observed, and the identity of the next speaker, the \emph{responder} B, is not given.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/motivation.pdf}
\caption{A Hi-EF test sample with several people on screen. Left: the groups into which \model{} sorts the
participants without future information (the speaker A of clip III, the most visible other participant L, and the
rest O). Right: summarising each clip around the speaker forecasts \emph{neutral}; keeping each participant as separate
evidence forecasts B's \emph{happy} response.}
\label{fig:motivation}
\end{figure}

The published model fuses each clip as a whole and then fuses the clips over time \citep{wang2026hief}. This is
natural for a two-party exchange, but Hi-EF is not one. In 47\% of the samples three or more people are on screen,
and the footage is edited in shot and reverse shot: the camera follows whoever talks and then cuts to the person who
listens. Speaker A and responder B share a frame in only 0.7\% of the clips, while B appears somewhere in clips I--III
in 88\% of the samples and is seen listening to A in 48\%. The evidence about the next speaker is therefore usually
in the input, but it sits in different frames from the speaker's and is diluted by any clip-level summary, which
follows the camera, the transcript and the audio, that is, the speaker (Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}).

Two regularities of conversation say why this evidence is valuable. Responders tend to take over the emotion of
their interlocutor \citep[emotional contagion;][]{hatfield1994contagion}, and individuals tend to stay in their own
recent emotional state \citep[emotional inertia;][]{kuppens2010inertia}. Both are properties of the individual and
both are visible in a participant's face before the participant speaks. This suggests a simple change of
perspective: the unit of evidence should be the participant in a turn, not the clip.

Making that change raises a practical question, because participants are not labelled. We find that they need not
be. Faces can be grouped into people within a sample, and the groups can be ordered by how much they appear, which
needs no identity, speaker label or knowledge of the future. A second question, whether the model must know \emph{who}
will respond, has a less expected answer. Giving it the true responder, a random co-present non-speaker, or no role
labels at all changes nothing measurable. What the forecast needs is that the faces of the people in the scene reach
the model as separate evidence next to the earlier turns. The model is participant-centric rather than
responder-centric. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A diagnosis of why clip-level fusion fits Hi-EF poorly: the benchmark is multi-party and shot-edited, and the
evidence about the next speaker is usually on screen but separated from the speaker's.
\item \model{}, a participant-centric forecaster of 0.71M parameters. Each participant in each turn becomes one
token, the participants are found without identity information, and a set encoder reads them with speech and scene
tokens. Under a preregistered single test read it improves UAR over the strongest published model, retrained on our
split, by 3.84 points and wins seven of eight test episodes.
\item Controlled analyses of what drives the gain. Co-present participants' faces and earlier turns are worth about
two UAR points each and the gain survives the removal of the end of the current turn, whereas participant identity,
role labels and extra encoder structure bring no measurable gain.
\item An error analysis that locates the limit: the advantage lies where the responder mirrors the speaker or keeps
their own emotion, and the missing information is the recognition of participants' emotional states.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Emotion Recognition and Forecasting in Conversation}

Emotion recognition in conversation labels utterances that are observed. Its models are speaker-centric: they keep a
state for each \emph{known} speaker, with memory networks or graphs
\citep{hazarika2018icon,majumder2019dialoguernn,ghosal2019dialoguegcn}, or fuse modalities with transformers
\citep{tsai2019mult}; multi-party benchmarks such as MELD supply speaker names \citep{poria2019meld}. Forecasting
the emotion of a turn that has not happened yet has been studied in text dialogue \citep{wang2020sentiment}. Hi-EF
\citep{wang2026hief} brings the task to multimodal video and withholds the responder's identity, which removes the
speaker tracking those models rely on. We keep the idea that individuals matter but drop the need to know who they
are: participants are grouped within a sample and treated as exchangeable evidence.

\subsection{Set Models and Interaction Analysis}

Attention over an unordered collection of elements with learned pooling seeds is formalised by the Set Transformer
\citep{lee2019set}; see also \citet{zaheer2017deep}. We use this view to describe \model{} and to test which structure
it needs. To measure whether a trained model uses cross-group interactions we follow EMAP \citep{hessel2020emap}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overall Architecture}

An MCIS (multilayered-contextual interaction sample) in Hi-EF \citep{wang2026hief} consists of four consecutive
clips from a TV series, each clip one subtitle utterance. Clips I and II are context, clip III is a turn of speaker
A, and clip IV is a turn of a different person B. The target is B's emotion in clip IV, one of seven classes
(angry, disgust, fear, happy, neutral, sad, surprise). Formally, given $X = (C_1, C_2, C_3)$, where each clip
$C_k$ has video frames, audio and a transcript, a forecaster outputs $p(y_B \mid X)$ over the seven classes. Clip IV
is never an input, B's identity is not given, and we do not assume that only two people take part.

\model{} is built on one principle: evidence is kept separate per participant and per turn until the encoder reads it
jointly. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} gives an overview and Algorithm~\ref{alg:rolenet} the forward pass. \model{} first
groups the visible faces into participants and sorts them into three slots: the speaker of the current turn, the
most visible other participant, and everyone else (Section~\ref{sec:roles}). It then summarises each participant in
each clip, and what is said and shown in each clip, by a single token (Section~\ref{sec:tokens}). The resulting set of
15 tokens is read jointly by a small set encoder, and a query token pools it into the forecast
(Section~\ref{sec:encoder}).

\subsection{Participant Grouping}
\label{sec:roles}

TV dialogue is usually edited as shot and reverse shot: the camera dwells on the speaker and then cuts to the person
who listens and will often answer. We use this convention to decide which participants to keep apart, without any
future information. Faces are detected in the frames of clips I--III at 4\,fps (at most 32 frames per clip) and
embedded with ArcFace \citep{deng2019arcface}. All face embeddings of an MCIS are clustered (average linkage, cosine
threshold 0.45), so that one cluster approximates one person across the three clips; clusters have no meaning outside
the sample and no name is attached to them. In clip III, the cluster with the most frames fills the speaker slot A
and the cluster with the second most frames fills the listener slot L; all other clusters share the slot O. No
listener is assigned when clip III shows a single person. Every face $f$ thus receives a slot
$r(f) \in \{A, L, O\}$. The rule reads neither clip IV nor speaker labels nor identity annotations. Slot L is a
proxy for the responder, but Section~\ref{sec:identity} shows that the model does not need it to be the responder.

\subsection{Evidence Tokens}
\label{sec:tokens}

Hi-EF clips last about two seconds, and a listener is often visible in only one to three frames. We therefore
summarise each participant slot in each clip by one token, which keeps the evidence set small while letting the model
weight the most expressive frame. Let $F_{r,k}$ be the frames (at most 24) of the faces in slot $r$ in clip $k$. Each
frame is described by a vector $\phi(f) \in \mathbb{R}^{146}$ that contains a 128-dimensional PCA of the HSEmotion
embedding \citep{savchenko2022hsemotion}, fitted on training faces only, its 8 expression probabilities and
valence/arousal, head pose, face box position and size, mouth opening, and the relative time in the clip. The frames
of a cell are pooled by attention:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
u_f &= \mathrm{MLP}(\phi(f)),\\
\alpha_f &= \operatorname{softmax}_{f \in F_{r,k}}\!\big(w^\top u_f\big),\\
x_{r,k} &= \textstyle\sum_f \alpha_f u_f + e^{\text{slot}}_r + e^{\text{clip}}_k ,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $e^{\text{slot}}_r$ and $e^{\text{clip}}_k$ are learned embeddings of the slot and the clip. A cell without any
face receives a learned placeholder $a_{r,k}$ instead of the pooled vector, which gives nine face tokens
$x_{r,k} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, $d = 128$, per MCIS.

What is said and the setting of each turn complement the faces. For each clip we build a speech token $s_k$ and a
scene token $g_k$:
\begin{align}
s_k &= W_t\,t_k + (W_a\,a_k)\,\delta_k + W_v\,v_k \nonumber\\ &\quad + e^{\text{speech}} + e^{\text{clip}}_k, \label{eq:sk}\\
g_k &= W_s\,[\bar c^{\,\text{frame}}_k;\,\bar c^{\,\text{face}}_k] + e^{\text{scene}} + e^{\text{clip}}_k, \label{eq:gk}
\end{align}
where $t_k$ is the CLIP text embedding of the transcript \citep{radford2021clip}, $a_k$ the AudioCLIP sound-event
vector \citep{guzhov2022audioclip} gated by an audio-found flag $\delta_k$, and $\bar c$ are mean CLIP embeddings of
the whole frames and of all faces. The 9-dimensional turn descriptor $v_k$ contains the mouth--audio synchrony of each
slot, the ECAPA \citep{desplanques2020ecapa} voice similarity to clip III and the number of identities; it tells the
model who is likely speaking in clips I and II without naming anyone. Together, the face, speech and scene tokens
form the evidence set $\mathcal{S} = \{x_{r,k}\} \cup \{s_k\} \cup \{g_k\}$ of 15 typed tokens.

\subsection{Set Encoding and Output}
\label{sec:encoder}

\subsubsection{Set Encoder}

The evidence set is read jointly, so that, for example, the listener's face in clip II can be combined with what A
says in clip III. We prepend a learned query token $q$ and apply two pre-norm set attention blocks
\citep[SAB;][]{lee2019set}, i.e.\ transformer layers \citep{vaswani2017attention} without positional encoding:
\begin{align}
H^{(0)} &= [q;\,\mathcal{S}], \nonumber\\
Z &= H^{(\ell-1)} + \mathrm{MHA}\big(\mathrm{LN}(H^{(\ell-1)})\big), \nonumber\\
H^{(\ell)} &= Z + \mathrm{FFN}(\mathrm{LN}(Z)),\quad \ell = 1, 2, \\
p(y_B \mid \mathcal{S}) &= \operatorname{softmax}\!\big(W\,\mathrm{LN}(h^{(2)}_q)\big),
\end{align}
with 4 heads, a 512-dimensional FFN and dropout 0.2. The forecast is read from the query position; because $q$ takes
part in self-attention, it acts as a pooling seed in the sense of PMA \citep{lee2019set}. The encoder is equivariant
to permutations of the typed tokens, and the order of the clips enters only through $e^{\text{clip}}$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\model{} forward pass for one MCIS}
\label{alg:rolenet}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require frames, transcripts and audio of clips I--III
\State Detect faces at 4\,fps; embed with ArcFace; cluster all faces of the MCIS into identities
\State $A \gets$ identity with most frames in clip III; $L \gets$ second most (or none); others $\to O$
\For{$r \in \{A, L, O\}$, $k \in \{1,2,3\}$}
  \State $x_{r,k} \gets \textsc{AttnPool}(F_{r,k})$, or $a_{r,k}$ if $F_{r,k} = \emptyset$
  \State $x_{r,k} \gets x_{r,k} + e^{\text{slot}}_r + e^{\text{clip}}_k$
\EndFor
\For{$k \in \{1,2,3\}$} \State build $s_k$ and $g_k$ (Eqs.~\ref{eq:sk}--\ref{eq:gk}) \EndFor
\State $H \gets [q;\, \{x_{r,k}\};\, s_{1:3};\, g_{1:3}]$ \Comment{16 tokens}
\For{$\ell = 1, 2$} \State $H \gets \mathrm{SAB}(H)$ \EndFor
\State \Return $\operatorname{softmax}(W\,\mathrm{LN}(H_q))$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Overall Learning Objectives}

Besides the cross-entropy $\mathcal{L}_B$ on $y_B$, three auxiliary heads predict $y_B$ from the mean of the face
tokens ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{face}}$), $y_B$ from the mean of the speech and scene tokens ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{ctx}}$),
and A's clip III emotion from A's clip III face token ($\mathcal{L}_{A}$). The overall loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_B + \lambda\,(\mathcal{L}_{\text{face}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{ctx}} + \mathcal{L}_{A}),
\end{equation}
with $\lambda = 0.3$. Modality dropout \citep{neverova2016moddrop} removes all speech and scene tokens with
probability 0.3, or else all face tokens with probability 0.15, so that the model is trained on random subsets of
$\mathcal{S}$. We use AdamW (learning rate $3\cdot10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$, batch 64, at most 80 epochs),
keep the checkpoint with the best UAR on five held-out training episodes (patience 12), and average the predicted
probabilities of several seeds.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We ask four questions. Does participant-centric evidence improve on the published model (Section~\ref{sec:main})?
Where does the gain come from (Section~\ref{sec:origin})? Does the model depend on who the participants are or on
their role labels, and does it need more structure (Section~\ref{sec:identity})? And where does it still fail
(Section~\ref{sec:analysis})?

\subsection{Datasets}

\paragraph{Hi-EF.} The released annotations give 2{,}830 MCIS from 53 episodes. To avoid shared clips between
partitions we split by episode (37/8/8 episodes; 1{,}993/428/409 MCIS for train/validation/test). Our numbers are
therefore not comparable to those printed in \citet{wang2026hief}, who used a different split; we retrain their
strongest configuration on ours. Classes are imbalanced: the 409 test MCIS contain six examples of \emph{fear}. To see
who is on screen, we grouped the faces of each sample into identities (ArcFace clustering, no annotation) and matched
voices with ECAPA, using clip IV for analysis only (Figure~\ref{fig:datastats}). Although the task is framed as a
two-party interaction, 47\% of the samples show three or more people. The responder is usually present before
speaking: B appears in clips I--III in 88\% of the samples, is seen listening in clip III in 48\%, and spoke in clip I
or II in 50\%. The editing alternates shot and reverse shot: A and B share a frame in only 0.7\% of clip III, so a
frame-level summary of a clip mixes different people. Turn-taking alone does not identify B, because the clip I/II
speaker is a third person in about 39\% of the samples. Reading the listener's face also carries signal: its zero-shot
facial expression forecasts B better than that of A ($+3.72$ UAR on 1{,}234 samples with a visible listener), while a
listener-presence flag alone does not.

\paragraph{MELD.} To test generalisation, we rebuilt Hi-EF-style windows from MELD \citep{poria2019meld}: three
turns, then a turn of a different speaker, giving 5{,}405/595/1{,}341 windows for train/dev/test. Speaker names are
not used as input.

\subsection{Settings, Metrics and Baseline}
\label{sec:baseline}

All design decisions use 5-fold cross-validation over the 45 training and validation episodes (2{,}421 MCIS), with
folds balanced by MCIS count. We report the UAR (macro recall, our main metric) and WAR (accuracy) of seed ensembles
under plain argmax scoring, following \citet{wang2026hief}, and assess differences with a bootstrap that resamples
seeds and episodes (2{,}000 draws). For structural comparisons with small effects we also report negative
log-likelihood (NLL) after temperature scaling \citep{guo2017calibration} on held-out training episodes. For the
test, \model{} and the baseline were trained on the 45 development episodes with five seeds, and the test split was read
once under a plan committed before the run (models, seeds, metrics and the order of confirmatory contrasts). The
preregistered primary metric was 7-class UAR after logit adjustment \citep{menon2021logit}; plain scoring was
registered as a descriptive analysis. Earlier, unrelated model families had been evaluated on the same test split;
Appendix~\ref{app:extra} lists every access. Apart from Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}, all
Hi-EF results use development CV only. On MELD, both models are trained with three seeds.

The baseline is the strongest configuration of \citet{wang2026hief}: per-clip temporal transformers over face and
frame features, cross-attention fusion with text and audio, and LSTM plus transformer fusion across clips. We retrain
it on our split from the released code (AdamW, learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch 32, 50 epochs, checkpoint by selection
UAR).

On the test split (Table~\ref{tab:main}), \model{} improves UAR by 3.84 points and WAR by 3.91 points over the baseline
and wins seven of the eight test episodes. Under logit adjustment, the preregistered primary metric, the gain is
similar ($+3.77$; 19.55 vs 23.31) but not significant. The main cause is the \emph{fear} class: with six test
examples, one correct \emph{fear} prediction is worth 2.38 UAR points, and logit adjustment pushes the baseline to
predict \emph{fear} for 27.6\% of the test MCIS (true rate 1.5\%), gaining two \emph{fear} hits while its \emph{angry}
recall falls to 1.3\%. Without \emph{fear}, the adjusted gain is $+9.95$. We therefore regard the test result as
consistent with the development evidence rather than as a confirmation of it, and we consider the choice of an
adjusted 7-class primary metric for a test set with six \emph{fear} examples a design error of our preregistration.
The gain comes with a much smaller model: \model{} has 0.71M parameters against 27.7M, and fuses 16 tokens at width 128
instead of 96 frames at width 512, about 40--75 times fewer fusion FLOPs (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}); the price is a
richer pre-processing that runs once per clip.

Is the gain only better features? Feeding the baseline architecture \model{}'s features, aggregated per clip as
usual, raises its UAR from 21.27 to 23.25, but \model{} stays 3.37 points ahead, significantly and in all five folds
(Table~\ref{tab:origin}). Pre-processing therefore explains only part of the gain. Most of the rest is associated
with the listener: without its tokens, \model{} is no longer distinguishable from the feature-matched baseline, and
the listener's tokens of clips I/II alone or of clip III alone each recover part of the margin while only both
together restore the full model. Evidence about the co-present participant thus enters through both the earlier
turns and the current one.

Is it only recognising a reaction that has already begun? About half of the listener's frames lie in the last fifth of
clip III, close to the target turn. We retrain \model{} with the end of clip III removed: faces after the cut are
discarded before the participants are grouped, and the scene token uses only the remaining frames, while the baselines
keep the full clip. With 80\% or 60\% of clip III, \model{} still exceeds the full-clip baseline by 4.46 and 3.81
points and the feature-matched baseline by 2.48 and 1.83 points, all significant, although the margins over the
feature-matched baseline are small. The listener's own contribution shrinks with the cut. When the listener is fixed to
the true responder, so that the cut removes frames but not the identity, about half of it survives without the late
frames; neither half is significant on its own. The forecast thus draws on a participant's earlier appearances and,
to a similar extent, on their reaction late in the current turn.

Removing what the model reads hurts. Without the face tokens UAR falls to 22.03, without the listener's tokens to
24.20 (in all five folds), and keeping only clip III gives 24.11. The two kinds of evidence are complementary: a
model that keeps only the speaker's face plus speech and scene tokens reaches 23.22, and the faces of the other
participants alone reach 23.10. Reading the tokens jointly matters too: slot paths combined additively reach 23.78.

Removing what labels the evidence does not. Dropping the slot embeddings (26.41), swapping the speaker and listener
slots in a random half of the samples (26.25, a difference of $-0.01$) or taking a random non-speaker of clip III as
listener (26.32) leaves UAR unchanged. Pooling all faces of a clip into one token reaches 25.80; in a fully matched
rerun, person tokens lead clip pooling by 1.0 point with and without auxiliary losses (5/5 and 4/5 folds) but not
significantly. A clip-pooled version of our own architecture still reaches 25.3 against 23.3 for the feature-matched
baseline (different runs), so much of the margin comes from the compact set encoder, and keeping participants apart
adds about one further point. What the model uses is the content of the faces, not the labels of the slots.

Nor does it need the right person. An oracle replaces the listener by the true responder: B is found as the dominant
face of clip IV and matched to an identity of clips I--III (cosine $\geq$ 0.55 and a clear margin over the
second-best identity for the groups of Table~\ref{tab:proxy}); this uses clip IV and serves only as an analysis. The
listener tokens help where the proxy is the responder ($+5.18$ UAR, significant) and not where it is someone else
($+0.02$), mostly because B is then seen only in clips I/II. Yet the oracle does not improve on the proxy in any group,
including the 215 MCIS where B is visible in clip III. In short, what matters is that the faces of the people in the
scene reach the model as separate evidence; which of them is called the listener does not.

Finally, the encoder does not need more structure (Table~\ref{tab:structure}). Making it exactly invariant to the
order of clips I and II, or of all three clips, does not hurt significantly. Factorial attention biases for temporal,
person and evidence-type relations are learned but bring no measurable gain, and neither do alternative readouts (mean
pooling, the listener's token, a read-only query or three queries). A group-level EMAP \citep{hessel2020emap} shows,
however, that the trained encoder does use non-additive interactions. We split the tokens into the listener (clips
I--III), the history (other tokens of clips I--II) and the current event (other tokens of clip III), and replace the
model by its best additive approximation over these groups. Held-out NLL rises significantly, by 0.011, and each group
interacts with the rest. The interactions are modest, about 5\% of the logit variance, and they do not involve the
transcript.

\subsection{Where Does It Fail?}
\label{sec:analysis}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/errors_by_group_cv.pdf}
\caption{Accuracy of \model{} by subgroup on development CV.}
\label{fig:groups}
\end{figure}

More than half of the correct forecasts are cases of mirroring. B's emotion equals A's clip III emotion in 35.5\% of
the development MCIS (34.7\% in our MELD windows). On these \emph{mirror} cases \model{} is correct 56.9\% of the time,
but only 27.7\% on \emph{non-mirror} cases, where it still predicts A's emotion 29\% of the time
(Figure~\ref{fig:groups}). Whether B will depart from A's emotion is partly predictable (AUC 0.70), but which emotion
follows is hardly predictable: recall on non-mirror cases is 1.6\% for \emph{disgust}, 2.8\% for \emph{surprise} and
0\% for \emph{fear}.

The responder's own inertia tells a similar story. In 27\% of the development MCIS B also spoke in clip I or II with a
labelled emotion (B found by voice in clip IV; analysis only), and in 48.9\% of these B keeps that emotion in clip IV.
In the run of Table~\ref{tab:origin}, \model{}'s advantage over the baseline is largest in these \emph{keep} cases:
56.6\% correct against 46.2\%, and 71.0\% against 55.9\% when the kept emotion also mirrors A. When B changes to
another emotion, all models are correct about 27\% of the time, and about 22\% when the new emotion also differs from
A's. Two factors cap accuracy: non-mirror cases, and labels the annotators marked as uncertain, where accuracy falls
from 40.5\% to 25.2\%.

The oracle analyses locate the missing information. With A's true clip III emotion added to \model{}'s outputs, the
UAR of a linear model on these outputs rises from 24.73 to 27.51 and NLL falls by 0.036; with A's emotion estimated by
a recogniser from clip III, the gain disappears. On the MCIS where B's own earlier turn is labelled, simply copying
that emotion gives 39.18 UAR against 27.39 for \model{}, and adding \model{}'s outputs to the label adds nothing
detectable. The missing signal thus lies in recognising participants' emotional states, which the fixed feature
extractors do poorly; none of the ways of recombining the same tokens that we tried recovered it. Frozen speech
\citep{wagner2023dawn} and text \citep{demszky2020goemotions} emotion encoders do not close the gap: in a linear probe
they raise the recognition of B's earlier turn only from 27.2 to 29.7 UAR and leave the forecast unchanged.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In multi-party video the person who is about to respond is usually already in view, and treating each participant in
each turn as separate evidence improves emotion forecasting over clip-level fusion with a model forty times smaller.
The gain does not depend on knowing who the participants are: the true responder, a random co-present participant and
the role labels of the slots all perform alike, and further encoder structure adds nothing measurable. The limit is
instead the recognition of participants' emotional states, in particular the responder's own earlier state. The rare
classes (\emph{fear}, \emph{surprise} and \emph{disgust}) mostly arise when the responder departs from the speaker's
emotion and remain unsolved; recognisers adapted to multi-party video and more examples of these reactions are the
next steps.

% ======================================================================================================
\section*{Limitations}

Our main claim rests on plain UAR, the benchmark metric, which was preregistered as a descriptive analysis; the
preregistered primary contrast, logit-adjusted UAR, was not significant. The test split had been used before for
unrelated model families (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}); the design of \model{} used development data only, and its test
numbers come from the single preregistered run. The test set is small: eight episodes give wide intervals, one
\emph{fear} example is worth 2.38 UAR points, and \model{} never forecasts \emph{fear} or \emph{surprise} correctly.
The design of \model{} was informed by analyses on the same development episodes used for cross-validation.

The benefit of keeping participants apart is the weakest part of the evidence: in a matched comparison, person tokens
lead clip pooling by about one UAR point, not significantly, and a clip-pooled version of our architecture already
captures much of the margin over the baseline. We therefore claim the participant-centric view as the setting in which
the other findings were obtained, not as an isolated cause of the gain. About half of the listener's frames fall in the
last fifth of clip III, close to the target turn. They are valid inputs under the benchmark protocol and carry part of
the listener's contribution: without them \model{} still beats the baselines, but the listener's own gain is no longer
significant (Section~\ref{sec:origin}), so part of it reflects an early reaction rather than forecasting further ahead.
The true responder used by the oracle is approximated by the dominant face of clip IV matched by face identity, so
matching errors would blur the comparison with the proxy; we did not verify the matches by hand. The same holds for
B's earlier emotion, which is known only where B's voice matches an earlier turn (27\% of the MCIS). Only 16\% of the
MCIS have two or more non-speakers in clip III, so the comparison of the proxy with a random listener has little power.
Text and audio come from general-purpose encoders (CLIP, AudioCLIP sound events), and conclusions about which evidence
matters are conditional on them. Frozen emotion-specific encoders improved recognition only slightly in a linear probe;
fine-tuned or conversation-specific recognisers were not tested. Hi-EF and MELD consist of scripted TV series with
shot and reverse-shot editing; the grouping rule relies on this style and may not transfer to fixed-camera recordings.
On MELD the gain is small and not significant, and the MELD test set was read twice: the first run failed on three
non-finite feature values, which were set to zero before the second run.

We use existing public benchmarks of scripted TV content. Face and voice embeddings are used only to group
observations within a short sample, and no identity is linked to a name. Forecasting a person's emotion could be
misused for manipulation or surveillance; our models are research artefacts with modest accuracy (about 25 UAR on
seven classes) and should not be used to make decisions about individuals.
