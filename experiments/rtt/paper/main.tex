\title{Watch the Listener: Participant-Centric Evidence\\for Emotion Forecasting in Multi-Party Video}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Emotion forecasting asks how a person will feel in the next conversational turn, before that turn is observed.
Existing multimodal models summarise each observed turn as a whole, so the evidence is dominated by whoever is
speaking, although in edited conversational video the person who answers is usually on screen already, listening.
We make the \emph{participant}, rather than the turn, the unit of evidence. \model{} groups the faces of a sample into
people without any identity information, summarises each person in each turn by one token, and reads these tokens
together with speech and scene tokens in a small set encoder (0.71M parameters). Evaluated once on the held-out test
split of a multi-party video benchmark under a preregistered plan, \model{} improves unweighted average recall over
the strongest published model, retrained on the same split with all its inputs, by 3.9 points; it is on par with the best
of seven retrained configurations of that model (+1.0, not significant) with about fifty times fewer parameters, and on a
second benchmark the differences are not significant. Controlled analyses show what matters and what does not. The
faces of co-present participants and the earlier turns are worth about two points each, whereas which participant is
taken as the likely responder, the true responder's identity and the role labels of the slots make no measurable
difference. The remaining errors lie in recognising participants' emotional states, not in how their evidence is
combined.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/motivation.pdf}
\caption{A multi-party conversation whose responder is still unobserved. The speaker A of the last observed turn
and a listener L are sorted into separate groups without future information. A model that summarises every turn as a
whole is dominated by the speaker and forecasts \emph{neutral}; keeping each participant as separate evidence
forecasts the responder's \emph{happy} reply. The next speaker is the listener L.}
\label{fig:motivation}
\end{figure}

Recognising an emotion that is displayed is a perception problem; forecasting one that has not yet been displayed is
a problem about people. Affective forecasting has a long history in psychology \citep{wilson2003affective}, and
recent work has begun to cast it as a learning problem on conversational video \citep{wang2026hief}: given the turns
of a conversation observed so far, predict the emotion of the person who speaks next. The task has two unusual
properties. The target turn is never observed, and neither is the identity of the next speaker, the
\emph{responder}.



Multimodal models for this task fuse each observed turn as a whole and then fuse the turns over time
\citep{wang2026hief}. This is natural for a two-party exchange, but conversations in films and television are often
multi-party and are edited in shot and reverse shot: the camera follows whoever talks and then cuts to the person who
listens. The evidence about the next speaker is therefore frequently in the input, since the responder is often in view
while somebody else speaks, but it sits in different frames from the speaker's. Any turn-level summary follows the
camera, the transcript and the audio, that is, the speaker, and dilutes it (Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}).

Two regularities of conversation say why this evidence is valuable. Responders tend to take over the emotion of
their interlocutor \citep[emotional contagion;][]{hatfield1994contagion}, and individuals tend to stay in their own
recent emotional state \citep[emotional inertia;][]{kuppens2010inertia}. Both are properties of the individual and
both are visible in a participant's face before the participant speaks. This suggests a change of perspective: the unit
of evidence should be the participant in a turn, not the turn.

Making that change raises a practical question, because participants are not labelled. We find that they need not
be. Faces can be grouped into people within a sample, and the groups can be ordered by how much they appear, which
needs no identity, speaker label or knowledge of the future. A second question, whether the model must know \emph{who}
will respond, has a less expected answer. Giving it the true responder, a random co-present non-speaker, or no role
labels at all changes nothing measurable. What the forecast needs is that the faces of the people in the scene reach
the model as separate evidence next to the earlier turns. The model is participant-centric rather than
responder-centric. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A diagnosis of why turn-level fusion fits multi-party, shot-edited video poorly: the evidence about the next
speaker is usually on screen but separated from the speaker's.
\item \model{}, a participant-centric forecaster of 0.71M parameters. Each participant in each turn becomes one
token, participants are found without identity information, and a set encoder reads them with speech and scene
tokens.
\item An evaluation on two multi-party video benchmarks against all published baselines of the original benchmark.
Under a preregistered single test read, \model{} improves UAR over the strongest published model, retrained on our
split with all its inputs, by 3.9 points; it is on par with the best of seven retrained configurations.
\item Controlled analyses of what drives the gain. Co-present participants' faces and earlier turns are worth about
two UAR points each, and the gain survives the removal of the end of the current turn, whereas participant identity,
role labels and extra encoder structure bring no measurable gain. An error analysis locates the limit in the
recognition of participants' emotional states.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Emotion Recognition and Forecasting in Conversation}

Emotion recognition in conversation (ERC) labels utterances that are observed \citep{poria2019emotion}. Classic models
are speaker-centric: they keep a state for each \emph{known} speaker with memory networks or graphs
\citep{hazarika2018icon,majumder2019dialoguernn,ghosal2019dialoguegcn,shen2021dagerc}. More recent work fuses text,
audio and video with correlation-aware attention \citep{shi2023multiemo} or unified multimodal models
\citep{hu2022unimse}, or builds multimodal large language models that recognise and explain emotion
\citep{cheng2024emotionllama,lian2025affectgpt}. PFA-ERC even
predicts pseudo-future context to help recognition \citep{khule2024pfaerc}. All of them label utterances that are
available, and multi-party benchmarks such as MELD \citep{poria2019meld} supply the speaker names that they exploit.
Forecasting the emotion of a turn that has not happened yet is much less studied. It has been addressed in text
dialogue \citep{wang2020sentiment,li2021emotion,koga2024forecasting}, where even large language models struggle
\citep{koga2024forecasting}, and in multimodal video by \citet{wang2026hief}, who withhold the responder's identity, which removes the speaker tracking
that ERC models rely on. We keep the idea that individuals matter but drop the need to know who they are: participants
are grouped within a sample and treated as exchangeable evidence, motivated by emotional contagion
\citep{hatfield1994contagion} and inertia \citep{kuppens2010inertia}.

\subsection{Multimodal Learning}

Multimodal learning combines complementary signals from several modalities \citep{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}.
Cross-modal transformers \citep{tsai2019mult}, shared embedding spaces \citep{radford2021clip,girdhar2023imagebind}
and, more recently, vision-language models that read images, video and text \citep{chen2024internvl,bai2025qwen25vl}
make strong frozen or pretrained encoders widely available; we follow the efficient recipe of a small fusion module on frozen
encoders. A recurring difficulty is that one modality dominates training, and balanced-learning methods counter it
by modulating gradients or re-learning the weaker modality \citep{wang2020makes,peng2022balanced,wei2024diagnosing};
modality dropout \citep{neverova2016moddrop} serves the same purpose, as do our auxiliary heads. Whether a trained
model uses interactions between modalities, rather than adding independent contributions, is hard to tell; we use the
EMAP analysis of \citet{hessel2020emap} on groups of participant tokens.

\subsection{Person-Centric Video and Social Interaction Modelling}

Several recent lines of work make the interacting people, rather than the frame or clip, the unit of modelling.
Ego-Exo4D, for instance, offers synchronised views of people performing skilled activities, as a basis for
understanding human behaviour from video \citep{grauman2024egoexo4d}. Dyadic generation models learn to produce a
listener's facial and body reaction from the speaker's behaviour \citep{ng2022learning}, with joint pretraining of
speaker and listener \citep{tran2024dim}, or with a multimodal language model that reads speech content, acoustics
and speaker emotion \citep{lai2025listening}. These works
show that a person's reaction is predictable from their partner's behaviour, but they generate behaviour for known
people and do not forecast the emotion of an unobserved next speaker in multi-party edited video. We transfer the
person-centric view to this setting without person tracks or identity: participants are formed by clustering faces
within the sample.

\subsection{Set-Based and Object-Centric Representations}

Permutation-invariant networks process unordered collections of elements \citep{zaheer2017deep}, and attention-based
variants add pooling by a learned seed \citep{lee2019set}. Object-centric models decompose a scene into a small set of
slots \citep{locatello2020slot}, which scales to real-world images when built on frozen self-supervised features
\citep{seitzer2023dinosaur}, and latent-bottleneck transformers read arbitrary inputs through a small set of latents
\citep{jaegle2022perceiverio}, building on the transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention}. \model{} follows this line of
work in spirit: it represents a conversation as a small typed set of participant, speech and scene tokens and reads it
with set attention, which makes it easy to test which parts of the set the forecast needs.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task Formulation}
\label{sec:task}

A conversation is a sequence of turns. We observe $T$ consecutive turns $X = (C_1, \dots, C_T)$, where each turn
$C_k = (V_k, a_k, w_k)$ consists of video frames $V_k$, audio $a_k$ and a transcript $w_k$. The last observed turn
$C_T$ is spoken by participant A. The next turn $C_{T+1}$ is spoken by a different participant, the responder B, and
is \emph{not} observed. The target is the emotion $y_B \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ expressed by B in $C_{T+1}$, with $K=7$
classes (angry, disgust, fear, happy, neutral, sad, surprise). The forecaster outputs $p(y_B \mid X)$.
The participants are neither labelled nor named: the model receives no speaker annotation or identity, B is not
specified, and more than two people may take part. Clips of a conversation are edited footage, so the people visible
in a turn need not be those who speak.

\subsection{Overall Architecture}

\model{} is built on one principle: evidence is kept separate per participant and per turn until the encoder reads
it jointly. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} gives an overview and Algorithm~\ref{alg:rolenet} the forward pass. \model{} first
groups the visible faces into participants and sorts them into three slots: the speaker of the last observed turn, the
most visible other participant, and everyone else (Section~\ref{sec:roles}). It then summarises each participant in
each turn, and what is said and shown in each turn, by a single token (Section~\ref{sec:tokens}). The resulting set of
tokens is read jointly by a set encoder, and a query token pools it into the forecast (Section~\ref{sec:encoder}).

\subsection{Participant Grouping}
\label{sec:roles}

Edited dialogue alternates between the speaker and the person who listens and will often answer. We use this
convention to decide which participants to keep apart, without any future information. Faces are detected in the
frames of the observed turns and embedded with a face-recognition network \citep{deng2019arcface}. All face embeddings
of a sample are clustered by average-linkage agglomerative clustering with a cosine threshold, so that one cluster
approximates one person across the turns; clusters have no meaning outside the sample and no name is attached to
them. In the last turn, the cluster with the most frames fills the speaker slot A and the cluster with the second
most frames fills the listener slot L; all other clusters share the slot O. No listener is assigned when the last turn
shows a single person. Every face $f$ thus receives a slot $r(f) \in \{A, L, O\}$. The rule reads neither the target
turn nor speaker labels nor identity annotations. Slot L is a proxy for the responder, but
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} shows that the model does not need it to be the responder.

\subsection{Evidence Tokens}
\label{sec:tokens}

A listener is often visible in only a few frames of a short turn. We therefore summarise each participant slot in each
turn by one token, which keeps the evidence set small while letting the model weight the most expressive frame. Let
$F_{r,k}$ be the frames of the faces in slot $r$ in turn $k$. Each frame is described by a vector $\phi(f)$ of facial
expression features (an expression embedding \citep{savchenko2022hsemotion}, expression probabilities,
valence/arousal), head pose, face position and size, mouth opening and the relative time in the turn. The frames of a
cell are pooled by attention:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
u_f &= \mathrm{MLP}(\phi(f)),\\
\alpha_f &= \operatorname{softmax}_{f \in F_{r,k}}\!\big(w^\top u_f\big),\\
x_{r,k} &= \textstyle\sum_f \alpha_f u_f + e^{\text{slot}}_r + e^{\text{turn}}_k ,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $e^{\text{slot}}_r$ and $e^{\text{turn}}_k$ are learned embeddings of the slot and the turn. A cell without any
face receives a learned placeholder $a_{r,k}$ instead of the pooled vector, which gives $3T$ face tokens $x_{r,k}$
per sample.

What is said and the setting of each turn complement the faces. For each turn we build a speech token $s_k$ and a
scene token $g_k$:
\begin{align}
s_k &= W_t\,t_k + (W_a\,a_k)\,\delta_k + W_v\,v_k \nonumber\\ &\quad + e^{\text{speech}} + e^{\text{turn}}_k, \label{eq:sk}\\
g_k &= W_s\,[\bar c^{\,\text{frame}}_k;\,\bar c^{\,\text{face}}_k] + e^{\text{scene}} + e^{\text{turn}}_k, \label{eq:gk}
\end{align}
where $t_k$ is the CLIP text embedding of the transcript \citep{radford2021clip}, $a_k$ a sound-event embedding
\citep{guzhov2022audioclip} gated by a flag $\delta_k$ that marks whether audio was found, and $\bar c$ are mean CLIP
embeddings of the whole frames and of all faces. The turn descriptor $v_k$ contains the mouth--audio synchrony of each
slot, the voice similarity \citep{desplanques2020ecapa} to the last turn and the number of identities; it tells the
model who is likely speaking in earlier turns without naming anyone. Together, the face, speech and scene tokens
form the evidence set $\mathcal{S} = \{x_{r,k}\} \cup \{s_k\} \cup \{g_k\}$ of $5T$ typed tokens.

\subsection{Set Encoding and Output}
\label{sec:encoder}

\subsubsection{Set Encoder}

The evidence set is read jointly, so that, for example, the listener's face in an earlier turn can be combined with
what A says in the last turn. We prepend a learned query token $q$ and apply $L$ pre-norm set attention blocks
\citep[SAB;][]{lee2019set}, i.e.\ transformer layers \citep{vaswani2017attention} without positional encoding:
\begin{align}
H^{(0)} &= [q;\,\mathcal{S}], \nonumber\\
Z &= H^{(\ell-1)} + \mathrm{MHA}\big(\mathrm{LN}(H^{(\ell-1)})\big), \nonumber\\
H^{(\ell)} &= Z + \mathrm{FFN}(\mathrm{LN}(Z)),\quad \ell = 1, \dots, L, \\
p(y_B \mid \mathcal{S}) &= \operatorname{softmax}\!\big(W\,\mathrm{LN}(h^{(L)}_q)\big).
\end{align}
The forecast is read from the query position; because $q$ takes part in self-attention, it acts as a pooling seed in
the sense of PMA \citep{lee2019set}. The encoder is equivariant to permutations of the typed tokens, and the order of
the turns enters only through $e^{\text{turn}}$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\model{} forward pass for one sample}
\label{alg:rolenet}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require frames, transcripts and audio of the observed turns $1..T$
\State Detect faces, embed them, and cluster all faces of the sample into identities
\State $A \gets$ identity with most frames in turn $T$; $L \gets$ second most (or none); others $\to O$
\For{$r \in \{A, L, O\}$, $k \in \{1..T\}$}
  \State $x_{r,k} \gets \textsc{AttnPool}(F_{r,k})$, or $a_{r,k}$ if $F_{r,k} = \emptyset$
  \State $x_{r,k} \gets x_{r,k} + e^{\text{slot}}_r + e^{\text{turn}}_k$
\EndFor
\For{$k \in \{1..T\}$} \State build $s_k$ and $g_k$ (Eqs.~\ref{eq:sk}--\ref{eq:gk}) \EndFor
\State $H \gets [q;\, \{x_{r,k}\};\, s_{1:T};\, g_{1:T}]$
\For{$\ell = 1..L$} \State $H \gets \mathrm{SAB}(H)$ \EndFor
\State \Return $\operatorname{softmax}(W\,\mathrm{LN}(H_q))$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Overall Learning Objectives}

Besides the cross-entropy $\mathcal{L}_B$ on $y_B$, three auxiliary heads predict $y_B$ from the mean of the face
tokens ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{face}}$), $y_B$ from the mean of the speech and scene tokens ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{ctx}}$),
and A's emotion in the last turn from A's face token ($\mathcal{L}_{A}$). The overall loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_B + \lambda\,(\mathcal{L}_{\text{face}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{ctx}} + \mathcal{L}_{A}).
\end{equation}
Modality dropout \citep{neverova2016moddrop} removes all speech and scene tokens, or else all face tokens, with a
fixed probability, so that the model is trained on random subsets of $\mathcal{S}$. At test time, the predicted
probabilities of several seeds are averaged.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}

\paragraph{Hi-EF.} Hi-EF \citep{wang2026hief} is a benchmark of multi-party conversational video from TV series. Its
2{,}830 samples (MCIS, multilayered-contextual interaction samples) from 53 episodes each consist of four consecutive
clips of about two seconds, each clip one subtitle utterance. Clips I and II are context, clip III ($T=3$) is a turn of
speaker A, and clip IV, the target, is a turn of a different person B; the emotion of B in clip IV is the label (seven
classes). Classes are imbalanced: the 409 test samples contain six examples of \emph{fear}. To see who is on
screen, we grouped the faces of each sample into identities (ArcFace clustering, no annotation) and matched voices
with ECAPA, using clip IV for analysis only (Figure~\ref{fig:datastats}). Although the task is framed as a two-party
interaction, 47\% of the samples show three or more people. The responder is usually present before speaking: B
appears in clips I--III in 88\% of the samples, is seen listening in clip III in 48\%, and spoke in clip I or II in
50\%. The editing alternates shot and reverse shot: A and B share a frame in only 0.7\% of clip III, so a clip-level
summary mixes different people. Turn-taking alone does not identify B, because the clip I/II speaker is a third person
in about 39\% of the samples. Reading the listener's face also carries signal: its zero-shot facial expression
forecasts B better than that of A ($+3.72$ UAR on 1{,}234 samples with a visible listener), while a listener-presence
flag alone does not.

\paragraph{MELD.} MELD \citep{poria2019meld} contains multi-party dialogues from a TV series, with an emotion label
per utterance in the same seven classes. It is designed for recognition in conversation, so we adapt it to forecasting
(Section~\ref{sec:setting}).

\subsection{Experimental Settings}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Splits and adaptation.} For Hi-EF we split by episode to avoid shared clips between partitions
(37/8/8 episodes; 1{,}993/428/409 samples for train/validation/test). The split printed in \citet{wang2026hief} is a
different, random one, so our numbers are not comparable to theirs; the strongest published configuration is therefore
retrained on our split. Design decisions use 5-fold cross-validation over the 45 training and validation episodes
(2{,}421 samples), with folds balanced by sample count. For MELD we build Hi-EF-style samples from every dialogue:
a window of four consecutive utterances (the first three are the observed turns, $T=3$) is kept when the fourth
utterance is spoken by a different person than the third, and its emotion label is the target. The official
train/validation/test dialogue partition is kept, giving 5{,}405/595/1{,}341 windows. The video, audio and transcript
of the three observed utterances are processed exactly as for Hi-EF; the fourth utterance and all speaker names are
never used as input.

\paragraph{Metrics.} The main metric is the unweighted average recall (UAR, macro recall), which is not affected by
class imbalance; we also report the weighted average recall (WAR, accuracy), as \citet{wang2026hief}. Both are computed
for seed ensembles under plain argmax scoring. Differences are assessed with a bootstrap that resamples seeds and
episodes (2{,}000 draws). For structural comparisons with small effects we also report the negative log-likelihood
(NLL) after temperature scaling \citep{guo2017calibration} on held-out training episodes.

\paragraph{Baselines.} We compare with all 15 configurations reported by \citet{wang2026hief}. They vary the visual
backbone (ResNet18, I3D, ViT), the visual inputs (face only, or face, body and scene), the modalities (video, plus
CLIP text and AudioCLIP audio), the fusion within a clip (average or transformer) and the fusion across clips
(average, transformer, or LSTM plus transformer). The eight ResNet18 and I3D configurations are end-to-end video
networks that need the raw videos and the authors' preprocessing, which is not released, so they are listed with the
numbers published for the original split (marked $\dagger$) and are not retrained. The seven ViT configurations work on
frozen CLIP features, and we retrain all of them, on our Hi-EF split and on MELD, with face, body and scene input as in
the paper (Section~\ref{sec:setting}, implementation details). Their best configuration (face, body and scene
features, transformer fusion with CLIP text and AudioCLIP audio, LSTM plus transformer across clips) is the
\emph{Baseline}. Our reimplementation of the intra-clip \emph{Average} fusion (mean over frames, then over branches and
modalities) and of the inter-clip \emph{Average} (mean of the three clip vectors) follows our reading of the paper, since
the released code has no separate class for them. Before the body features existed we had also trained the Baseline
without the body branch in the preregistered run; it is listed separately. As multimodal large language model
baselines we add Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct \citep{bai2025qwen25vl}, zero-shot and few-shot, which reads the three clips as video
with their subtitles but not the audio (Appendix~\ref{app:llm}). In the analyses we also use a feature-matched version of
the Baseline, fed with \model{}'s features aggregated per clip.

\paragraph{Implementation details.} Faces are detected at 4\,fps (at most 32 frames per clip, at most 24 per slot
cell) and embedded with ArcFace; clustering uses average linkage with a cosine threshold of 0.45. A frame is
described by a 146-dimensional vector: a 128-dimensional PCA of the HSEmotion embedding (fitted on training faces
only), 8 expression probabilities and valence/arousal, and geometric features. Tokens have width $d=128$; the turn
descriptor $v_k$ has 9 dimensions and voices count as the same speaker above an ECAPA cosine of 0.35. The set encoder
has $L=2$ pre-norm SABs, 4 heads, a 512-dimensional FFN and dropout 0.2, and reads 15 tokens plus the query. Auxiliary
heads have weight $\lambda=0.3$; modality dropout removes all speech and scene tokens with probability 0.3, or else all
face tokens with probability 0.15. We use AdamW (learning rate $3\cdot10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$, batch 64, at
most 80 epochs) and keep the checkpoint with the best UAR on five held-out training episodes (patience 12). The
retrained ViT baselines use AdamW (learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-5}$, batch 32, 50 epochs, checkpoint by
selection UAR, as in the replication of the paper's code). Their body branch is not in the released features, so we add it:
16 evenly spaced frames per clip, the largest person detected in each frame by a YOLOv8-small detector
(confidence $\geq$ 0.4, height $\geq$ 64 px), and the CLIP ViT-B/32 features of the crop, the same encoder as the face and
scene features. \model{} does not use these features. On
Hi-EF, development results use 10 seeds; for the test, \model{} and the Baseline without body branch were trained on the
45 development episodes with five seeds, and the test split was read once under a plan committed before the run (models,
seeds, metrics and the order of confirmatory contrasts). The preregistered primary metric was 7-class UAR after logit
adjustment \citep{menon2021logit}; plain scoring was registered as a descriptive analysis. The later runs (seven ViT
baselines with body branch, large language model) read the test split again, and earlier, unrelated model families had
been evaluated on it too; every access is listed in Appendix~\ref{app:extra}. On MELD, all models are trained with three
seeds. Appendix~\ref{app:extra} lists all hyper-parameters, and Appendix~\ref{app:split} describes the splits.

Table~\ref{tab:main} gathers all results. On the Hi-EF test split, \model{} improves UAR by 3.94 points (95\% interval
$[1.22, 7.91]$, bootstrap over test episodes) and WAR by 5.13 points over the Baseline, the benchmark's best
configuration retrained on our split with all its inputs, and wins six of the eight test episodes. The Baseline lies
close to the numbers published for the same configuration (23.72 UAR, 35.19 WAR on their split), which suggests that the
retraining is faithful; adding the body branch did not change it (21.30 against 21.40 without). \model{} is also
significantly better than three further retrained configurations (ViT with face, body and scene only: $+3.63$
$[0.49, 8.14]$; with text and no audio: $+4.42$; with average intra-clip fusion and LSTM plus transformer across clips:
$+6.23$). It is not distinguishable from the other three: the audio-only variant ($+2.52$ $[-0.06, 6.80]$), the all-average
variant ($+2.40$) and the configuration with transformer intra-clip fusion and averaged clips ($+1.01$
$[-1.98, 4.60]$), which is the best retrained configuration on test, and it is also better than the Baseline ($+2.93$ $[1.17, 5.50]$). It
is the best configuration on the early-stopping episodes of both datasets as well, so a choice on development data would
have picked it; the comparison with it is therefore the fair, demanding one, and on it the two models are on par. In our
runs the three-layer LSTM across clips hurts: the five configurations with LSTM average 20.0 UAR against 22.0 for the two
without, whereas LSTM plus transformer was the best fusion in the benchmark paper. This may reflect our reimplementation,
the smaller training set or the split, and not the benchmark's design. Under logit
adjustment, the preregistered primary metric, the gain over the Baseline without body branch is similar ($+3.77$; 23.31 vs
19.55) but not significant, and so is the gain over the Baseline with body ($+0.75$; 22.56). The main cause is the
\emph{fear} class: with six test examples, one correct \emph{fear} prediction is worth 2.38 UAR points, and logit
adjustment pushes the Baseline without body branch to predict \emph{fear} for 27.6\% of the test samples (true rate
1.5\%); without \emph{fear}, the adjusted gain over it is $+9.95$. We therefore regard the test result as consistent with
the development evidence rather than as a confirmation of it. The gain comes with a much smaller model: \model{} has
0.71M parameters against 34.1M, and fuses 16 tokens at width 128 instead of 144 frames at width 512, about 65--110 times
fewer fusion FLOPs (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}); the price is a richer pre-processing that runs once per clip.
\todo{Qwen2.5-VL-7B results: one paragraph on zero-shot and few-shot (UAR/WAR, share of \emph{neutral} predictions, effect of the examples).}

On MELD, \model{}'s UAR is higher than the Baseline's (16.85 against 15.27) and than all other retrained configurations,
but the differences are not significant (against the Baseline $+1.58$ $[-0.35, 3.90]$; against the best retrained
configuration, 16.42, $+0.43$). Five of the seven retrained configurations obtain a WAR above 45 by predicting
\emph{neutral} for most windows, whereas \model{} spreads its forecasts. MELD is dominated by \emph{neutral} (48\% of the
test windows), so WAR rewards the majority-class collapse that UAR penalises. All models are close to chance, so MELD
shows that the setting is hard rather than that the approach transfers.

\section{Ablation Study and Analysis}
\label{sec:ablation}

All analyses in this section use development cross-validation on Hi-EF only. The Baseline in them is the benchmark's
best configuration without the body branch (the runs predate the body features; on test the body branch did not change
it, Table~\ref{tab:main}). We ask three questions: where does the gain come from,
what does the model not need, and does it need more structure? A last part analyses its errors.

Is the gain only better features? Feeding the Baseline \model{}'s features, aggregated per clip as usual, raises its UAR
from 21.27 to 23.25, but \model{} stays 3.37 points ahead, significantly and in all five folds
(Table~\ref{tab:origin}). Pre-processing therefore explains only part of the gain. Most of the rest is associated with
the listener: without its tokens, \model{} is no longer distinguishable from the feature-matched Baseline, and the
listener's tokens of clips I/II alone or of clip III alone each recover part of the margin while only both together
restore the full model. Evidence about the co-present participant thus enters through both the earlier turns and the
current one.

Is it only recognising a reaction that has already begun? About half of the listener's frames lie in the last fifth of
clip III, close to the target turn. We retrain \model{} with the end of clip III removed: faces after the cut are
discarded before the participants are grouped, and the scene token uses only the remaining frames, while the baselines
keep the full clip. With 80\% or 60\% of clip III, \model{} still exceeds the full-clip Baseline by 4.46 and 3.81
points and the feature-matched Baseline by 2.48 and 1.83 points, all significant, although the margins over the
feature-matched Baseline are small. The listener's own contribution shrinks with the cut and is no longer
significant on its own. The forecast thus draws on a participant's earlier appearances and, to a similar extent, on
their reaction late in the current turn.

The encoder does not need more structure (Table~\ref{tab:structure}). Making it exactly invariant to the order of
clips I and II, or of all three clips, does not hurt significantly. Factorial attention biases for temporal, person and
evidence-type relations are learned but bring no measurable gain, and neither do alternative readouts (mean pooling,
the listener's token, a read-only query or three queries). A group-level EMAP \citep{hessel2020emap} shows, however,
that the trained encoder does use non-additive interactions. We split the tokens into the listener (clips I--III), the
history (other tokens of clips I--II) and the current event (other tokens of clip III), and replace the model by its
best additive approximation over these groups. Held-out NLL rises significantly, by 0.011, and each group interacts
with the rest. The interactions are modest, about 5\% of the logit variance, and they do not involve the transcript.

\subsection{Where Does It Fail?}
\label{sec:analysis}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/errors_by_group_cv.pdf}
\caption{Accuracy of \model{} by subgroup on development CV.}
\label{fig:groups}
\end{figure}

More than half of the correct forecasts are cases of mirroring. B's emotion equals A's clip III emotion in 35.5\% of
the development samples (34.7\% in our MELD windows). On these \emph{mirror} cases \model{} is correct 56.9\% of the
time, but only 27.7\% on \emph{non-mirror} cases, where it still predicts A's emotion 29\% of the time
(Figure~\ref{fig:groups}). Whether B will depart from A's emotion is partly predictable (AUC 0.70), but which emotion
follows is hardly predictable: recall on non-mirror cases is 1.6\% for \emph{disgust}, 2.8\% for \emph{surprise} and
0\% for \emph{fear}.

The responder's own inertia tells a similar story. In 27\% of the development samples B also spoke in clip I or II
with a labelled emotion (B found by voice in clip IV; analysis only), and in 48.9\% of these B keeps that emotion in
clip IV. In the run of Table~\ref{tab:origin}, \model{}'s advantage over the Baseline is largest in these \emph{keep}
cases: 56.6\% correct against 46.2\%, and 71.0\% against 55.9\% when the kept emotion also mirrors A. When B changes
to another emotion, all models are correct about 27\% of the time, and about 22\% when the new emotion also differs
from A's. Two factors cap accuracy: non-mirror cases, and labels the annotators marked as uncertain, where accuracy
falls from 40.5\% to 25.2\%.

The oracle analyses locate the missing information. With A's true clip III emotion added to \model{}'s outputs, the
UAR of a linear model on these outputs rises from 24.73 to 27.51 and NLL falls by 0.036; with A's emotion estimated by
a recogniser from clip III, the gain disappears. On the samples where B's own earlier turn is labelled, simply copying
that emotion gives 39.18 UAR against 27.39 for \model{}, and adding \model{}'s outputs to the label adds nothing
detectable. The missing signal thus lies in recognising participants' emotional states, which the fixed feature
extractors do poorly; none of the ways of recombining the same tokens that we tried recovered it. Frozen speech
\citep{wagner2023dawn} and text \citep{demszky2020goemotions} emotion encoders do not close the gap: in a linear probe
they raise the recognition of B's earlier turn only from 27.2 to 29.7 UAR and leave the forecast unchanged.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In multi-party video the person who is about to respond is usually already in view, and treating each participant in
each turn as separate evidence improves emotion forecasting over turn-level fusion with a model about fifty times smaller.
The gain does not depend on knowing who the participants are: the true responder, a random co-present participant and
the role labels of the slots all perform alike, and further encoder structure adds nothing measurable. The limit is
instead the recognition of participants' emotional states, in particular the responder's own earlier state. The rare
classes (\emph{fear}, \emph{surprise} and \emph{disgust}) mostly arise when the responder departs from the speaker's
emotion and remain unsolved; recognisers adapted to multi-party video and more examples of these reactions are the
next steps.

% ======================================================================================================
\section*{Limitations}

The test split of the main benchmark is small (eight episodes; six \emph{fear} examples), the preregistered
logit-adjusted contrast was not significant, and the gain on MELD is not significant. The benefit of keeping
participants apart, over pooling each clip, is about one UAR point and not significant; much of the margin over the
baseline comes from the compact set encoder. The grouping rule relies on shot and reverse-shot editing and may not
transfer to fixed-camera recordings, and the features come from general-purpose encoders. The best of the seven retrained
ViT configurations (also the best on the early-stopping episodes) is not significantly below \model{}, the eight ResNet18 and
I3D configurations are reported with their published numbers on a different split and not retrained, and the language
model does not read audio. Details of every test access are in Appendix~\ref{app:extra}.

We use public benchmarks of scripted TV content. Face and voice embeddings are used only to group observations
within a short sample, and no identity is linked to a name. Forecasting a person's emotion could be misused for
manipulation or surveillance; our models are research artefacts with modest accuracy (about 25 UAR on seven classes)
and should not be used to make decisions about individuals.

\section{Data Splits}
\label{app:split}

\paragraph{Hi-EF.} The benchmark holds 2{,}830 multilayered-contextual interaction samples from 53 episodes of TV
series. Consecutive samples of an episode share clips (the target clip of one sample can be an observed clip of the next),
so partitions must not cut through an episode. We therefore split by episode, with the assignment fixed in a file (seed 42)
before any model was trained: 37 episodes (1{,}993 samples) for training, 8 (428) for validation and 8 (409) for test
(Figure~\ref{fig:split_hief}a). The three partitions have similar class distributions (Figure~\ref{fig:split_hief}b), and
\emph{fear} has six test samples. Design decisions use only the 45 training and validation episodes. In development
cross-validation these episodes are divided into five folds of whole episodes, balanced by sample count, and every model is
trained five times, each time on four folds and evaluated on the fifth (Figure~\ref{fig:split_hief}c). For the test run,
a model is fitted on 40 of the 45 episodes and early-stopped on the other five, which are drawn once with
\texttt{random.Random(2026)} (episodes 08, 09, 26, 39 and 49) and shared by all models; the test episodes are not used
for any choice. The split printed in the benchmark paper is a different, random 70/30 split over samples, so its numbers
are not comparable with ours.

\paragraph{MELD.} MELD has no forecasting task, so we turn each dialogue into samples of the same form
(Figure~\ref{fig:split_meld}a). A window is four consecutive utterances $u_{i-3}, \dots, u_i$ of one dialogue; the first
three are the observed turns and the emotion label of $u_i$ is the target. A window is kept only if the speaker of $u_i$
differs from the speaker of $u_{i-1}$, as the next speaker differs from the last observed one in the benchmark. The
official partition of MELD into training, development and test dialogues is kept, so no dialogue and no
utterance appears in two partitions. The rule gives 5{,}405 / 595 / 1{,}341 windows (the training set loses one window whose video
cannot be decoded) from 821 / 95 / 223 dialogues; \emph{neutral} makes up 47\% of the test windows (Figure~\ref{fig:split_meld}b).
Utterances are processed as Hi-EF clips: 16 frames per video, CLIP features of the whole frame and of the face, AudioCLIP
audio features, CLIP text features of the transcript, and the same face and voice grouping. The target utterance and the
speaker names are never an input. Training uses the training windows, early stopping the development windows, and the
test windows are read at the end; the models are trained with three seeds.

\section{Large Language Model Baselines}
\label{app:llm}

We evaluate Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct \citep{bai2025qwen25vl}, a vision-language model, without any training on our data
(Figure~\ref{fig:llm}). The same protocol is used on Hi-EF and on MELD.

\paragraph{Input.} For each sample the three observed clips are given as three videos of eight evenly spaced frames,
resized to $336 \times 252$ pixels, each followed by the subtitle of the clip; the model has no audio input. The target clip
and the speaker names are not shown. The instruction is fixed before the test run, written once and tested on validation
rows only:

\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
You are shown three consecutive clips (I, II, III) of a conversation from a TV series, each with its subtitle. A different
person than the speaker of clip III will speak in the next clip, which is not shown. Predict which emotion that next speaker
will express. Answer with exactly one word from: angry, disgust, fear, happy, neutral, sad, surprise.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Zero-shot and few-shot.} In the zero-shot setting the prompt is the instruction followed by the three clips. In the
few-shot setting it is preceded by seven worked examples, one per emotion class, in a random but fixed class order. An example
consists of the same instruction, the subtitles and the middle frame of each of its three clips (a still image, to keep the
prompt short), and the true emotion of the next speaker as the assistant's answer. Examples are drawn from the training rows of
the same dataset (Hi-EF: the training and validation episodes; MELD: the training windows), never from test, and we use three
independent example sets (seeds 0, 1 and 2); we report the mean over the sets.

\paragraph{Scoring.} We do not let the model generate text. At the position of the answer, we read the logits of the first
token of each of the seven label words (\texttt{ang}, \texttt{dis}, \texttt{f}, \texttt{happy}, \texttt{neutral}, \texttt{sad},
\texttt{sur}; all distinct) and apply a softmax over the seven values. This gives a seven-class distribution without parsing
and makes the run deterministic; UAR and WAR are computed from its argmax as for the other models. Because four labels are
split into word pieces, the first-token probability is only an approximation of the label probability, which can penalise
\emph{fear} (a single letter). The model runs in half precision on two 16-GB GPUs; one sample takes about 3.6 seconds in the
zero-shot setting.
