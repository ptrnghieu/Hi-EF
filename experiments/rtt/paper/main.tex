\title{Watch the Listener: Identity-Free Responder Modeling\\for Emotion Forecasting in Multi-Party Video}

\begin{abstract}
Emotion forecasting asks how a person will feel in the next conversational turn, before that turn is observed.
In the Hi-EF benchmark the future responder is not identified at inference time, and existing models summarise
the preceding clips around whoever is speaking. We argue that the responder is usually already on screen,
listening, and that a forecaster should be built around that person. We present \model{}, which (i) selects a
prospective responder without any future information and (ii) represents the preceding clips as a set of
participant-by-turn evidence tokens read by a set encoder. On the locked Hi-EF test split, evaluated once under a
preregistered plan, \model{} improves unweighted average recall over the strongest published configuration by
3.84 points and wins seven of eight test episodes; the preregistered primary contrast under logit-adjusted scoring
was not significant, which we trace to a test set with six examples of one class. Replacing our responder proxy by
the true responder identity changes nothing, whereas removing the listener's face or the preceding context each
costs about two points. Explicit temporal, relational or role structure in the encoder brings no measurable gain.
Remaining errors concentrate on turns where the responder does not mirror the speaker's emotion.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recognising an emotion that is displayed is a perception problem; forecasting an emotion that has not yet been
displayed is a problem about people. Affective forecasting has a long history in psychology
\citep{wilson2003affective}, and \citet{wang2026hief} recently turned a version of it into a benchmark:
given three consecutive clips of a TV conversation (I--III), predict the emotion of the person who speaks in clip IV.
Two properties make the task unusual. The target clip is never observed, and the identity of the future speaker
(the \emph{responder}, B) is not given.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/motivation.pdf}
\caption{A Hi-EF test sample with several people on screen. Left: the roles that \model{} assigns without future
information, i.e.\ the speaker A of clip III, the listener L chosen as responder proxy and the others O. Right:
summarising each clip around the speaker forecasts \emph{neutral}; \model{}, built around the listener, forecasts
B's \emph{happy} response.}
\label{fig:motivation}
\end{figure}

Existing systems for this setting process each clip as a whole and fuse the clips over time
\citep{wang2026hief}. The clip III speaker (A) dominates such summaries, because the camera, the transcript and the
audio follow whoever talks. Yet the person who will answer is often visible already, listening to A
(Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}). Two regularities of conversation suggest that this person matters most. Responders
tend to take over the emotion of their interlocutor \citep[emotional contagion;][]{hatfield1994contagion}, and
individuals tend to stay in their own recent emotional state \citep[emotional inertia;][]{kuppens2010inertia}. Both
pass through the responder: the first is visible as a reaction while listening, the second as the responder's own
earlier expressions.

We therefore build the forecaster around the responder, although the responder is unknown. Our contributions are
as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item We propose an identity-free responder proxy: within the current turn, the second most visible person after
the speaker is taken as the listener, and all evidence is organised around this listener, the speaker and everyone
else. No future information, speaker label or identity annotation is used.
\item We represent the preceding clips as a participant-centric evidence set, in which each participant in each turn
contributes one token built from their face frames, alongside one speech and one scene token per turn. A small set
encoder reads the tokens jointly and a query token pools them into a forecast. We call the model \model{}; it has
0.71M parameters.
\item We evaluate under a strict protocol: all design decisions use episode-level cross-validation, and the locked
test split was read once under a preregistered plan. \model{} improves unweighted average recall (UAR) over the
strongest configuration of \citet{wang2026hief}, retrained on the same split, by 3.84 points and wins seven of eight
test episodes.
\item Controlled analyses explain the gain: the proxy loses nothing against the true responder, the listener's face
and the preceding context are each worth about two UAR points, the encoder needs no explicit temporal order or
relation biases, and the remaining errors lie in recognising conversational states before a shift.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Emotion Recognition and Forecasting in Conversation}

Emotion recognition in conversation labels utterances that are observed, typically with speaker-aware memory or
graph models \citep{hazarika2018icon,majumder2019dialoguernn,ghosal2019dialoguegcn} and multimodal transformers
\citep{tsai2019mult}; multi-party benchmarks such as MELD provide speaker names \citep{poria2019meld}.
DialogueRNN keeps a state per party, updated by utterances \citep{majumder2019dialoguernn}, and assumes that party
identities are known. Forecasting the sentiment or emotion of a turn that has not happened yet has been studied in
text dialogue \citep{wang2020sentiment}. Hi-EF \citep{wang2026hief} brings the task to multimodal video with an
unidentified responder, which removes the speaker-tracking machinery that recognition models rely on. We instead
select the prospective responder from what is visible in the current turn, and show that knowing the true responder
adds nothing on Hi-EF.

\subsection{Set Models and Interaction Analysis}

Attention over an unordered collection of elements with learned pooling seeds is formalised by the Set Transformer
\citep{lee2019set}; see also \citet{zaheer2017deep}. We use this view to describe \model{} and to test which structure
it needs. To measure whether a trained model uses cross-group interactions we follow EMAP \citep{hessel2020emap}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overall Architecture}

An MCIS (multilayered-contextual interaction sample) in Hi-EF \citep{wang2026hief} consists of four consecutive
clips from a TV series, each clip one subtitle utterance. Clips I and II are context, clip III is a turn of speaker
A, and clip IV is a turn of a different person B. The target is B's emotion in clip IV, one of seven classes
(angry, disgust, fear, happy, neutral, sad, surprise). Formally, given $X = (C_1, C_2, C_3)$, where each clip
$C_k$ has video frames, audio and a transcript, a forecaster outputs $p(y_B \mid X)$ over the seven classes. Clip IV
is never an input, B's identity is not given, and we do not assume that only two people take part.

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} gives an overview of \model{}, and Algorithm~\ref{alg:rolenet} its forward pass. \model{} first
assigns every visible face to one of three roles: the speaker of the current turn, a listener who serves as a proxy
for the responder, and everyone else (Section~\ref{sec:roles}). It then summarises each person in each clip, and
what is said and shown in each clip, by a single token (Section~\ref{sec:tokens}). The resulting set of 15 tokens is
read jointly by a small set encoder, and a query token pools it into the forecast (Section~\ref{sec:encoder}).

\subsection{Role Assignment}
\label{sec:roles}

TV dialogue is usually edited as shot and reaction shot: the camera dwells on the speaker and then cuts to the
person who listens and will answer. We use this convention to guess the responder without any future information.
Faces are detected in the frames of clips I--III at 4\,fps (at most 32 frames per clip) and embedded with ArcFace
\citep{deng2019arcface}. All face embeddings of an MCIS are clustered (average linkage, cosine threshold 0.45), so
that one cluster approximates one person across the three clips. In clip III, the cluster with the most frames is
taken as the speaker A and the cluster with the second most frames as the listener L; all other clusters are treated
as others O, and no listener is assigned when clip III shows a single person. Every face $f$ thus receives a role
$r(f) \in \{A, L, O\}$. The rule reads neither clip IV nor speaker labels nor identity annotations, and
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} shows that replacing L by the true responder does not improve the forecast.

\subsection{Evidence Tokens}
\label{sec:tokens}

Hi-EF clips last about two seconds, and a listener is often visible in only one to three frames. We therefore
summarise each person in each clip by one token, which keeps the evidence set small while letting the model weight
the most expressive frame. Let $F_{r,k}$ be the frames (at most 24) of the faces with role $r$ in clip $k$. Each
frame is described by a vector $\phi(f) \in \mathbb{R}^{146}$ that contains a 128-dimensional PCA of the HSEmotion
embedding \citep{savchenko2022hsemotion}, fitted on training faces only, its 8 expression probabilities and
valence/arousal, head pose, face box position and size, mouth opening, and the relative time in the clip. The frames
of a cell are pooled by attention:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
u_f &= \mathrm{MLP}(\phi(f)),\\
\alpha_f &= \operatorname{softmax}_{f \in F_{r,k}}\!\big(w^\top u_f\big),\\
x_{r,k} &= \textstyle\sum_f \alpha_f u_f + e^{\text{role}}_r + e^{\text{clip}}_k ,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $e^{\text{role}}_r$ and $e^{\text{clip}}_k$ are learned embeddings of the role and the clip. A cell without any
face receives a learned placeholder $a_{r,k}$ instead of the pooled vector, which gives nine face tokens
$x_{r,k} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, $d = 128$, per MCIS.

What is said and the setting of each turn complement the faces. For each clip we build a speech token $s_k$ and a
scene token $g_k$:
\begin{align}
s_k &= W_t\,t_k + (W_a\,a_k)\,\delta_k + W_v\,v_k \nonumber\\ &\quad + e^{\text{speech}} + e^{\text{clip}}_k, \label{eq:sk}\\
g_k &= W_s\,[\bar c^{\,\text{frame}}_k;\,\bar c^{\,\text{face}}_k] + e^{\text{scene}} + e^{\text{clip}}_k, \label{eq:gk}
\end{align}
where $t_k$ is the CLIP text embedding of the transcript \citep{radford2021clip}, $a_k$ the AudioCLIP sound-event
vector \citep{guzhov2022audioclip} gated by an audio-found flag $\delta_k$, and $\bar c$ are mean CLIP embeddings of
the whole frames and of all faces. The 9-dimensional turn descriptor $v_k$ contains the mouth--audio synchrony of each
role, the ECAPA \citep{desplanques2020ecapa} voice similarity to clip III and the number of identities; it tells the
model who is likely speaking in clips I and II without naming anyone. Together, the face, speech and scene tokens
form the evidence set $\mathcal{S} = \{x_{r,k}\} \cup \{s_k\} \cup \{g_k\}$ of 15 typed tokens.

\subsection{Set Encoding and Output}
\label{sec:encoder}

\subsubsection{Set Encoder}

The evidence set is read jointly, so that, for example, the listener's face in clip II can be combined with what A
says in clip III. We prepend a learned query token $q$ and apply two pre-norm set attention blocks
\citep[SAB;][]{lee2019set}, i.e.\ transformer layers \citep{vaswani2017attention} without positional encoding:
\begin{align}
H^{(0)} &= [q;\,\mathcal{S}], \nonumber\\
Z &= H^{(\ell-1)} + \mathrm{MHA}\big(\mathrm{LN}(H^{(\ell-1)})\big), \nonumber\\
H^{(\ell)} &= Z + \mathrm{FFN}(\mathrm{LN}(Z)),\quad \ell = 1, 2, \\
p(y_B \mid \mathcal{S}) &= \operatorname{softmax}\!\big(W\,\mathrm{LN}(h^{(2)}_q)\big),
\end{align}
with 4 heads, a 512-dimensional FFN and dropout 0.2. The forecast is read from the query position; because $q$ takes
part in self-attention, it acts as a pooling seed in the sense of PMA \citep{lee2019set}. The encoder is equivariant
to permutations of the typed tokens, and the order of the clips enters only through $e^{\text{clip}}$. Self-attention
adds pairwise interactions between tokens in every layer; Section~\ref{sec:ablation} shows that the order of the
clips can be removed without loss and that the trained model does use these interactions.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\model{} forward pass for one MCIS}
\label{alg:rolenet}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require frames, transcripts and audio of clips I--III
\State Detect faces at 4\,fps; embed with ArcFace; cluster all faces of the MCIS into identities
\State $A \gets$ identity with most frames in clip III; $L \gets$ second most (or none); others $\to O$
\For{$r \in \{A, L, O\}$, $k \in \{1,2,3\}$}
  \State $x_{r,k} \gets \textsc{AttnPool}(F_{r,k})$, or $a_{r,k}$ if $F_{r,k} = \emptyset$
  \State $x_{r,k} \gets x_{r,k} + e^{\text{role}}_r + e^{\text{clip}}_k$
\EndFor
\For{$k \in \{1,2,3\}$} \State build $s_k$ and $g_k$ (Eqs.~\ref{eq:sk}--\ref{eq:gk}) \EndFor
\State $H \gets [q;\, \{x_{r,k}\};\, s_{1:3};\, g_{1:3}]$ \Comment{16 tokens}
\For{$\ell = 1, 2$} \State $H \gets \mathrm{SAB}(H)$ \EndFor
\State \Return $\operatorname{softmax}(W\,\mathrm{LN}(H_q))$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Overall Learning Objectives}

Besides the cross-entropy $\mathcal{L}_B$ on $y_B$, three auxiliary heads predict $y_B$ from the mean of the face
tokens ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{face}}$), $y_B$ from the mean of the speech and scene tokens ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{ctx}}$),
and A's clip III emotion from A's clip III face token ($\mathcal{L}_{A}$). The overall loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_B + \lambda\,(\mathcal{L}_{\text{face}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{ctx}} + \mathcal{L}_{A}),
\end{equation}
with $\lambda = 0.3$. Modality dropout \citep{neverova2016moddrop} removes all speech and scene tokens with
probability 0.3, or else all face tokens with probability 0.15, so that the model is trained on random subsets of
$\mathcal{S}$. We use AdamW (learning rate $3\cdot10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$, batch 64, at most 80 epochs),
keep the checkpoint with the best UAR on five held-out training episodes (patience 12), and average the predicted
probabilities of several seeds.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}

\paragraph{Hi-EF.} The released annotations give 2{,}830 MCIS from 53 episodes. To avoid shared clips between
partitions we split by episode (37/8/8 episodes; 1{,}993/428/409 MCIS for train/validation/test). Our numbers are
therefore not comparable to those printed in \citet{wang2026hief}, who used a different split; we retrain their
strongest configuration on ours (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}). Classes are imbalanced: the 409 test MCIS contain six
examples of \emph{fear}. To see who is on screen, we grouped the faces of each sample into identities (ArcFace
clustering, no annotation) and matched voices with ECAPA, using clip IV for analysis only
(Figure~\ref{fig:datastats}). Although the task is framed as a two-party interaction \citep{wang2026hief}, Hi-EF is
multi-party in practice: 47\% of samples show three or more people. The responder is usually present before it
speaks: B appears in clips I--III in 88\% of samples, is seen listening in clip III in 48\%, and spoke in clip I or
II in 50\%. The editing alternates shot and reaction shot: A and B share a frame in only 0.7\% of clip III, so a
frame-level summary of a clip mixes different people. Turn-taking alone does not identify B, because the clip I/II
speaker is a third person in about 39\% of samples. Reading the listener's face also carries signal: the zero-shot
facial expression of the listener forecasts B better than that of A ($+3.72$ UAR on 1{,}234 samples with a visible
listener), while a listener-presence flag alone does not.

\paragraph{MELD.} To test generalisation, we rebuilt Hi-EF-style windows from MELD \citep{poria2019meld}: three
turns, then a turn of a different speaker, giving 5{,}405/595/1{,}341 windows for train/dev/test. Speaker names are
not used as input.

\subsection{Experimental Settings}

All design decisions use 5-fold cross-validation over the 45 training and validation episodes (2{,}421 MCIS), with
folds balanced by MCIS count. We report the UAR of seed ensembles and assess differences with a bootstrap that
resamples seeds and episodes (2{,}000 draws). For the test, \model{} and the baseline were trained on the 45
development episodes with five seeds, and the test split was read once under a plan committed before the run
(models, seeds, metrics and the order of confirmatory contrasts). Earlier, unrelated model families had been
evaluated on the same test split; Appendix~\ref{app:extra} lists every access. On MELD, both models are trained with
three seeds, using the training windows for fitting, the dev windows for early stopping and the test windows for the
comparison. Hyper-parameters are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:extra}.

\subsection{Evaluation Criteria}

Following \citet{wang2026hief}, we report UAR (macro recall), our main metric, and WAR (accuracy) under plain argmax
scoring. The preregistered primary test metric was 7-class UAR after logit adjustment \citep{menon2021logit} of the
seed-averaged probabilities, with plain scoring as a descriptive analysis. For controlled comparisons on
development data we also report negative log-likelihood (NLL) after temperature scaling \citep{guo2017calibration}
on held-out training episodes.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baseline}

Our baseline is the strongest configuration of \citet{wang2026hief}: per-clip temporal transformers over face and
frame features, cross-attention fusion with text and audio, and LSTM plus transformer fusion across clips. We retrain
it on our split as re-implemented from the released code (AdamW, learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch 32, 50 epochs,
checkpoint by selection UAR). As the best configuration reported for Hi-EF, it is our reference.

\model{} is also much cheaper than the baseline (Table~\ref{tab:complexity}). For a set of $n$ tokens of width $d$,
an SAB costs $O(n^2 d + n d^2)$; \model{} fuses a fixed $n = 16$ tokens at $d = 128$, so the encoder cost is constant

transformers over 16 frames at $d = 512$ in each of the three clips, followed by cross-attention fusion, a
three-layer LSTM and a transformer over the clips. \model{} therefore uses about 40 times fewer parameters and 40--75
times fewer fusion FLOPs. The price is a richer pre-processing, computed once per clip and shared by every MCIS that
contains it.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows that removing or replacing any component of \model{} lowers UAR. The face tokens
matter most: without them UAR falls from 25.92 to 22.03. Among the faces, the listener is the key. Removing only the
listener's tokens lowers UAR from 26.24 to 24.20, in all five folds, and the loss is concentrated on MCIS where a
listener is visible in clip III. The preceding context matters as much: keeping only clip III gives 24.11, and most of
the loss comes from clip II. The two kinds of evidence are complementary: a speaker-centric model that keeps only A's
face and the speech and scene tokens reaches 23.22, and the faces of the listener and the others alone reach 23.10.
Processing all tokens jointly matters too: separate role paths combined additively reach 23.78, and an interaction
term recovers only part of the gap. Role labels, speech and scene tokens and the auxiliary training signals have
smaller effects.

We next ask whether the forecaster needs to know who responds. An oracle replaces the listener by the true
responder: B is found as the dominant face of clip IV and matched to an identity of clips I--III, which is possible
for 88\% of MCIS; this uses clip IV and serves only as an analysis. The proxy's listener equals the matched responder
in 68\% of the MCIS where the proxy assigns a listener. Table~\ref{tab:proxy} shows no difference between proxy and
oracle, also on the 390 MCIS where the proxy picks the wrong person. The model uses what the visible faces show, and
an identity-free selection of the evidence suffices.

Finally, we test whether the encoder needs more structure than a set encoder (Table~\ref{tab:structure}). Making it
exactly invariant to the order of clips I and II, or of all three clips (verified numerically, maximum logit change
$1.4\cdot10^{-6}$), does not hurt significantly. Factorial attention biases for temporal, person and evidence-type
relations are learned but bring no gain, and neither do alternative readouts (mean pooling, the listener's token, a
read-only query or three queries). A group-level EMAP \citep{hessel2020emap} shows, however, that the trained encoder
does use non-additive interactions. We split the tokens into the listener (clips I--III), the history (other tokens
of clips I--II) and the current event (other tokens of clip III), and replace the model by its best additive
approximation over these groups. Held-out NLL rises significantly, by 0.011, and each group interacts with the rest.
The interactions are modest, about 5\% of the logit variance, and they do not involve the transcript: an additive
split of text versus everything else loses nothing.

\subsection{Visualization}
\label{sec:analysis}

Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} shows where the gain comes from. The baseline confuses polarity: it forecasts \emph{happy}
for 58\% of \emph{sad} responses and recalls only 3\% of them. \model{} reduces this confusion to 18\% and raises
\emph{sad} recall to 23\%, while keeping \emph{angry}, \emph{happy} and \emph{neutral} at a similar level. Neither
model forecasts \emph{fear} or \emph{surprise} correctly, and \emph{disgust} only rarely; we return to this
limitation in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

Most successful forecasts are cases of mirroring. B's emotion equals A's clip III emotion in 35.5\% of development
MCIS (34.7\% in our MELD windows). On these \emph{mirror} cases \model{} is correct 56.9\% of the time, but only
27.7\% on \emph{shifts}, where it still predicts A's emotion 29\% of the time; the listener's tokens help mostly on
mirror cases. Whether a shift will happen is partly predictable (AUC 0.70), but which emotion follows a shift is
not: recall on shifts is 1.6\% for \emph{disgust}, 2.8\% for \emph{surprise} and 0\% for \emph{fear}.

The errors are of two kinds (Figure~\ref{fig:groups}). Some are objective: on a shift the next emotion is not
determined by what is visible, and accuracy halves. Others are subjective: when the annotators marked B's label as
uncertain, accuracy falls from 40.5\% to 25.2\%, a ceiling set by label ambiguity rather than by the model. Accuracy
does not depend on whether a listener is visible in clip III (38.0\% either way), which is consistent with the model
falling back on context and on the speaker when the listener is absent.

Two oracle analyses show that the missing information is a recognition problem. With A's true clip III emotion added
to \model{}'s outputs, UAR rises from 24.73 to 27.51 and NLL falls by 0.036; with A's emotion estimated by a
recogniser from clip III, the gain disappears. On the 26\% of MCIS where B's own earlier turn is labelled, simply
copying B's earlier emotion gives 39.07 UAR against 27.98 for \model{}, and adding \model{}'s outputs to that label
adds nothing detectable. The useful signal is the participants' current emotional states, which the fixed feature
extractors recognise poorly; better ways of combining the same tokens cannot recover it.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Forecasting a responder's emotion on Hi-EF is mostly a matter of looking at the right person. An identity-free
listener proxy and a participant-centric evidence set read by a small set encoder outperform the published
configuration on a locked test split, the proxy loses nothing against the true responder, and additional
structure in the encoder does not help. The remaining errors point to recognising the participants' current
emotional states, especially before a shift, as the next problem to solve.

Two classes remain unsolved: \model{} recalls none of the \emph{fear} and \emph{surprise} responses on test, and only
2.5\% of \emph{disgust}. These classes are rare and mostly arise as shifts away from the speaker's emotion, where the
visible evidence says little about the next state. In future work we will address them with better recognition of
the participants' current states, for example emotion- and prosody-aware encoders, and with more training examples
of these reactions.

% ======================================================================================================
\section*{Limitations}

Our main claim rests on plain UAR, the benchmark metric, which was preregistered as a descriptive analysis; the
preregistered primary contrast, logit-adjusted UAR, was not significant. The test split had been used before for
unrelated model families (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}); the design of \model{} used development data only, and its test
numbers come from the single preregistered run. The test set is small: eight episodes give wide intervals, one
\emph{fear} example is worth 2.38 UAR points, and \model{} never forecasts \emph{fear} or \emph{surprise} correctly.
The design of \model{} was informed by analyses on the same development episodes used for cross-validation.

About half of the listener frames fall in the last fifth of clip III, close to the target turn. They are valid
inputs under the benchmark protocol, but part of the gain may come from recognising the responder's early reaction
rather than from forecasting further ahead. The true responder used by the oracle is approximated by the dominant
face of clip IV matched by face identity, so matching errors would blur the proxy--oracle comparison. Text and audio
come from general-purpose encoders (CLIP, AudioCLIP sound events), not from emotion- or prosody-specific models, and
conclusions about which evidence matters are conditional on them. Hi-EF and MELD consist of scripted TV series with
shot/reaction-shot editing; the listener proxy relies on this editing style and may not transfer to fixed-camera
recordings. On MELD the gain is small and not significant, and the MELD test set was read twice: the first run failed
on three non-finite feature values, which were set to zero before the second run.

We use existing public benchmarks of scripted TV content. Face and voice embeddings are used only to group
observations within a short sample, and no identity is linked to a name. Forecasting a person's emotion could be
misused for manipulation or surveillance; our models are research artefacts with modest accuracy (about 25 UAR on
seven classes) and should not be used to make decisions about individuals.
